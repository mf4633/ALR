of EPA SWMM Output Using Adaptive Vortex Particle Methods."* It contains:

## 1. Worked-example section (draft for the paper)

### N. Application: Post-Processing an EPA SWMM Model

To demonstrate the post-processor end-to-end, a five-node EPA SWMM 5 model
(three sub-catchments draining a 30-acre urban watershed through four
rectangular open-channel conduits to a free outfall) was routed under a
two-hour design storm (peak inflow 85 cfs). The SWMM `.out` binary was then
read directly and every junction was screened with the vortex-particle (ALR)
turbulence method (300 particles per node), with no additional meshing or CFD
software. The entire screen over the full time series completed in
approximately two seconds on a laptop.

For each node the method extracts the peak-of-time-series hydraulic state and
reports the induced-velocity maximum, the bed shear stress inferred from the
resolved Reynolds stress, the Shields parameter, and — for a specified bed
material — the logistic scour-severity flag. Table N ranks the five nodes on a
gravel bed by peak bed shear.

| Rank | Node | Width (ft) | Peak Q (cfs) | V_max (fps) | Bed shear (psf) | Shields | Screen |
|------|------|-----------|--------------|-------------|-----------------|---------|--------|
| 1 | J4   | 5.0 | 79.9 | 7.8 | 1.61 | 0.47 | HIGH |
| 2 | J2   | 4.0 | 50.8 | 6.6 | 1.30 | 0.39 | HIGH |
| 3 | OUT1 | 6.0 | 76.4 | 6.8 | 1.25 | 0.37 | HIGH |
| 4 | J3   | 5.0 | 84.6 | 6.2 | 1.16 | 0.34 | HIGH |
| 5 | J1   | 4.0 | 30.0 | 4.6 | 0.63 | 0.19 | MODERATE |

*Table N. Turbulence screen of the SWMM network on a 10 mm gravel bed, ranked
by peak bed shear. The Shields parameter is the physical incipient-motion
discriminator (motion begins near θ ≈ 0.03–0.06); every node here exceeds it,
and the screen ranks severity, isolating the downstream confluence J4 as the
governing location and the headwater J1 as the mildest.*

The screen correctly identifies the steep downstream confluence (J4) as the
governing scour location — it carries the highest bed shear despite not having
the largest peak discharge, because its narrower section and steeper energy
grade concentrate the near-bed turbulence. Node J1, the headwater junction,
is the only location approaching a stable threshold. This ordering is the
practical output an engineer wants from a screening pass: *where in the network
to look first*, produced directly from an existing SWMM model.

**Figure N.** (a) Node inflow hydrographs from the SWMM run that drive the
screen; (b) peak bed shear ranked by node (bars), with the Shields parameter
(discriminating) and the logistic risk index (saturated) on the secondary
axis, against the incipient-motion reference. *(See `swmm_example.png`.)*

## 2. Revision notes — derived-index saturation

The worked example surfaced two issues that should be reconciled before the
paper's scour-severity claims are read as ranking tools. **Neither affects the
underlying hydraulics** (velocity, bed shear, Shields), which are correct and
discriminate well; both concern *derived* indices layered on top.

### 2.1 The logistic scour-severity index saturates

The logistic index (paper §2.4) is centred on an empirical design shear
(0.10 psf for sand). Realistic urban-conduit bed shears here are 0.6–1.6 psf,
giving excess-shear ratios of 6–16, which drive the logistic to exactly 1.000
at **every** node on sand — i.e. it cannot rank a normal drainage network. It
only begins to discriminate on coarser beds (on gravel, J1 falls to 0.86 while
the rest remain ≈1.0).

**Recommendation.** Present the logistic index honestly as a **binary screening
flag** ("above design shear → screen further"), not as a severity *ranking*,
and do the ranking with the continuous Shields parameter / bed shear. This is
what the draft section above does. Alternatively, re-centre the logistic on a
shear that actually spans the design range of interest, and show a case where
it varies across nodes.

### 2.2 The scour-depth potential is capped

`scour_depth_potential` (Meyer-Peter-Müller × assumed 100 annual flow-hours)
hits its hard-coded 10 ft/yr cap at every wet node here, so it too provides no
discrimination as reported. Either raise/remove the cap and report the raw
MPM rate, or drop this column from the screening table and keep it only for
single-node design output.

> These are consistent with the earlier internal findings that the derived
> risk metrics needed care (see `REVIEW_FINDINGS_round2.md`); the hydraulics
> themselves are sound.

## 3. Reproducibility notes ("Code Availability")

The paper states the benchmark/validation suite is reproducible with one
command. On a clean machine two problems block that today:

1. **`pyswmm` will not install** on current Linux/macOS: its transitive
   `julian` dependency fails to build against modern `setuptools`
   (`AttributeError: install_layout`). The headless SWMM runner imports
   `from pyswmm import Output`, so it cannot run as shipped.
2. **Stale `.out` files raise "Error 440"** when read by a different SWMM
   engine version than wrote them.

`run_swmm_example.py` avoids both: it reads output through **`swmm-toolkit`**
directly (which installs cleanly and ships the engine), regenerates the `.out`
from the `.inp` so versions always match, and depends on nothing else beyond
`numpy`/`matplotlib`.

**Recommended fixes for the repo/paper:**
- Pin `swmm-toolkit` (not `pyswmm`) as the required reader, or gate the
  `pyswmm` import so `swmm-toolkit` is the default path.
- Ship models as `.inp` and run the engine on demand rather than distributing
  version-locked `.out` files.
- Reconcile the "109 tests" claim with `VALIDATION_REPORT.txt`, which currently
  documents only 3.
